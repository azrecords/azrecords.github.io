what got you into music production? how long have you been doing it for?
</div>

<div class="interviewee">
<font size="5" color="#6358cc">crxw</font> <font color="#AAAAAA">2026-09-23T15:52:12.912Z</font><br>
</div>
<div class="crxw-answer">
i think i started because at the time, not much really interested me. not games, not school (i was constantly skipping it lol), not really people either. then i stumbled across this new wave of music (mostly idm and vocaloid), and remember being kind of in awe at the emotions it made me feel, because they felt completely new to me.
<br><br>
that gave me this idea that music could potentially make you feel things you’d never felt before, and that really excited me. novelty is probably one of the things that excites me most about life in general.
<br><br>
when i started, i basically pushed everything else aside for about a year just to learn and understand music production. i was doing it every day for around 6–10 hours. i felt more attracted to it than anything else i’d ever done, mostly because it felt like there was endless potential in it.
<br><br>
i started around four years ago now, i think. funnily enough, the second track i ever worked on in my life is still up on my profile,"<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VMWSe0Ipdqg"><u>eeried</u></a>."

<br><br>
do you have any particular overarching workflow/process that follows a track from start to finish? how long may that take? do you focus on one project at a time, or maybe multiple?
</div>

<div class="interviewee">
<font size="5" color="#6358cc">crxw</font> <font color="#AAAAAA">2026-09-23T18:21:33.740Z</font><br>
</div>
<div class="crxw-answer">
i have a few rules i set for myself. one of the biggest is that i don’t want to use any preexisting sounds or presets, so pretty much everything has to be made specifically for the track.
<br>
i usually start with a piano composition and let my ear guide me rather than trying to force everything into a specific theoretical framework. i’m really into jazz, especially unconventional harmony and the way it can approach tension and resolution, so working by ear fits pretty naturally with how i want the music to feel. once the composition starts taking shape, i’ll create all the synthesizer sounds around that particular idea, still mostly judging everything by ear and whether it belongs in that world.
<br>
i also try not to reuse sounds i’ve made for older tracks, even if i originally made them from scratch. i like every project having its own little vocabulary of sounds rather than pulling from a library of things i already know work. 
</div>

<br><br>
coming off of sound synthesis, are there any pieces of gear (plugins, hardware, so on) you'd like to note as particularly influential/reliable in your craft?
</div>

<div class="interviewee">
<font size="5" color="#6358cc">crxw</font> <font color="#AAAAAA">2026-09-24T18:21:52.690Z</font><br>
</div>
<div class="crxw-answer">
i think i’m deeply influenced by the idea of machines becoming human, or somehow turning into living organisms, so the yamaha fs1r is definitely one of my favorite synths. there’s something about its formant synthesis and the way it can move between sounding synthetic, vocal, and almost organic that fits perfectly with what i’m trying to make. for example, the choir in “downright resolution” was made entirely with the fs1r.
<br><br>
before i could really get into hardware though, serum was probably the most important synth for me. i’ve used it for so long that i know pretty much all the ins and outs of it, so at this point i can usually imagine a sound and already have a pretty good idea of how to build it.
</div>

you're quite eccentric when it comes to naming tracks. are there any lesser-known (or outright unguessable) meanings to some of the titles you'd like to share?
</div>

<div class="interviewee">
<font size="5" color="#6358cc">crxw</font> <font color="#AAAAAA">2026-09-25T19:19:02.667Z</font><br>

<div class="crxw-answer">
tbh, they’re usually pretty unconscious. it’s always about trying to reveal something about myself, something i felt in the moment, or just words that came to me naturally. some of them do have deeper meanings and a certain world built around them, but i usually think it’s more interesting to let them speak for themselves.
<br><br>
i like letting the listener understand whatever they want to understand from them, and if someone is really invested, they can try to decrypt what’s behind everything. it’s usually written in a pretty poetic way rather than being something i’d want to explain directly
</div>

<br><br>
you're got two particularly star-studded releases: what were those bursts of popularity like? how do you feel about "unpledged allegiance" now, in retrospect?
</div>

<div class="interviewee">
<font size="5" color="#6358cc">crxw</font> <font color="#AAAAAA">2026-09-29T00:32:44.001Z</font><br>
</div>
<div class="crxw-answer">
i think those releases are a phase of my work that i still really respect, but i didn’t care that much about the fact that they reached a lot of people. what matters more to me in retrospect is what they represented at the time
<br><br>
“i love the way it cries” is a funny one because i never expected it to become popular at all. the song was actually supposed to be scrapped from the album initially.
<br><br>
with “unpledged allegiance” especially, i still value it, but i don’t think it has the amount of depth i eventually wanted my work to have. there were ideas in it that i still think are cool, i just don’t think i had fully figured out how to express them yet. i see it as an important phase, but not something that represents the full scope of what i am now.
</div>

do you have some favorite albums, or recent ones in your rotation, that you'd like to speak of? is genre discovery and diversity a priority to you in your music listening habits?
</div>

i really love jazz and unconventional harmony in general. some of my favorite albums would probably be john coltrane’s giant steps, ahmad jamal’s the awakening, and max roach’s members, don’t git weary.

when it comes to electronic music, i listen to a lot of dennoko-p and koronba, and then the classics like squarepusher and aphex twin.
<br>
i think genre diversity is really important because, no matter the artist or genre, there’s usually something i can learn from it. listening to things outside of what i normally make helps me see music from a different perspective, which really helps 🙂
